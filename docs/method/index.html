Method

How we score car reliability

Look up your car

  1. Complaints: the full NHTSA owner-complaint file (1,607,537 vehicle complaints, 1995 to October 2026).
  2. Sales: U.S. sales per model per year from automakers' published figures. Tesla doesn't report by model, so we use published estimates and say so.
  3. Same window for every car: a complaint counts only if it was filed within 3 years of the model year.
  4. Rate: complaints per 100,000 cars sold, matched model year to sales year. When a new model's first cars sold the calendar year before its model year (that year shows almost no complaints), those sales are counted with the first model year. Since 10-24.
  5. Index: each car's rate against the average car of the same model years. 100 = average, 50 = half the complaints, 300 = triple.

What it doesn't measure

A complaint is a safety complaint filed with the government, not a repair record. Fleet and rental-heavy models have fewer owners filing. Luxury lessees often go straight to the dealer. Some owners file more readily than others. Read it as early-life safety complaints, not total cost of ownership.

Also on every car page

  • Recalls: every NHTSA safety recall campaign for that model year (NHTSA recalls API).
  • Crash tests: NHTSA 5-star safety ratings for each body style tested (model years 2011 on).
  • Fuel economy: EPA combined mpg and yearly fuel cost from fueleconomy.gov, across the versions sold that year.
  • When problems show up: the odometer reading owners gave in their complaints. Unlike the score, this uses every complaint for the model year, because high-mileage failures come years later. Readings of 0 or over 400,000 miles are left out.
  • How serious: complaints in which the owner reported a crash, a fire, an injury, a death or a tow. Owners report these; NHTSA doesn't confirm the cause.

Using the data

The rankings are free to cite with a link to lemonindex.com (CC BY 4.0). The raw complaint data is public at NHTSA.
